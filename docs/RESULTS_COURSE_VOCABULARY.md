# Results in the vocabulary of the lecture notes (Jaeger & de Jong, *Neural Networks (AI)*, v1.3.3)

Section numbers refer to the notes. Numbers come from `DEVLOG.md` (entries 4–15). Terms that are **ours** (not in the notes) are marked, so they can be defined in the report instead of presented as textbook terms.

## 1. The learning problem (Sec. 1.1–1.2, 2.2)
- **Supervised learning task.** Training sample S = (u_i, y_i): the input pattern u_i is the Whisper-small encoder output for one utterance (300 grouped frames × 3840, frozen and pre-computed), the target y_i is the transcript (token sequence). 12,000 utterances of LibriSpeech train.clean.100 (about 42% of that split, first 12,000 in stream order).
- **Model and hypothesis space H.** f̂ = (frozen LM) ∘ h_θ ∘ (frozen encoder). Only h_θ is trained, so H = {f̂_θ}, θ ∈ R^D with D ≈ 10.0 M: a **multilayer perceptron** with one hidden layer (3840 → 2048 → 1024, ReLU) as the main model.
- **Loss function.** Training uses the **cross-entropy loss** (eq. 4, generalised to a multi-class softmax output over the vocabulary) on the transcript tokens. The task metric, word error rate (WER), is a **counting loss** (cf. eq. 2) on words that is not differentiable, so it is only used for evaluation, not for gradient descent.
- **Empirical risk / training error** R^emp (eq. 6) = mean training loss. **Risk** R (eq. 5) is estimated on held-out data: the validation set V (100 utterances of validation.clean) during training and the test set S_test (the first 500 utterances of test.clean) at the end.

## 2. Baselines and flexibility (Sec. 1.3–1.4)
- **Elementary baseline.** A single linear layer (3840 → 1024), the simplest model class. Test WER 20.3% ± 0.8 (3 seeds) against 18.8% ± 0.4 for the MLP (3 seeds): the bootstrap intervals overlap, so the data do not separate them. Reference points: the untrained projectors (MLP 168.8%, linear 117.0%; the LM hallucinates) and Whisper-small with its own decoder (4.5%, a different system, not a baseline in the strict sense).
- **Flexibility (THE curve, Fig. 6).** The MLP is the more flexible class, the linear layer the less flexible one, but it is not a strict subset (ReLU hidden layer of width 2048), so this is not a clean model class inclusion sequence (1.4.1). Both end at similar risk, which places us on the flat part of THE curve: more flexibility buys little here.
- **Overfitting.** Training and validation loss stay close in all runs (entry 4), so we have **no evidence of overfitting** in the budgets we ran (1,500 steps ≈ half an epoch of the 12,000 utterances; the long run is 12,000 steps ≈ 4 epochs). As the notes insist, we do not *know* this beyond the train/validation comparison.

## 3. Regularization (Sec. 1.4.2)
- Our **penalty term** is `α² · reg` with reg = mean (‖projected frame‖ − mean text-embedding norm)² and α² = `norm_penalty`. Unlike the L2/weight-decay regularizer of the notes (reg(θ) = Σ w²) it penalizes the **norm of the model's output** rather than of its parameters, so it acts on the function, not directly on θ.
- **Effect when α² is increased:** α² = 0 → test WER 18.8%, final validation loss 0.375; α² = 0.1 → WER 29.6% ± 7.9, validation loss 0.527 ± 0.013; α² = 1 and 10 stay at the plateau for the 1,500-step budget (WER 208% and 118%, no usable model); α² = 1 does reach a low loss after about 1,500–1,900 steps. This is the behaviour the notes describe for large α²: the regularization term starts to dominate and the model "does not care anymore about the training data".
- **Interpretation in the notes' terms.** The model is not overfitting to begin with, so regularizing moves us to the left of the optimum on the flexibility axis: training and validation risk both rise and no gain in generalisation appears. The reason to regularize here is therefore not generalisation but a question about **representation** (Sec. 6 below).

## 4. Optimisation: performance surface, plateau, learning rate (Sec. 2.2.3)
- Training is **gradient descent on a performance surface** R^emp(θ) (we use a variant with adaptive step size, AdamW, plus **minibatches** of 4 utterances; the notes mention such adaptive schemes as a reason deep learning became trainable). Small random **initialization** of θ.
- **Learning rate (adaptation rate) and stability vs speed.** Without gradient clipping the drop step scales as lr^-0.5 (three learning rates, one seed each); this relation does not survive with clipping. Gradient clipping (norm 1.0) moved the drop from step ~1,490 to ~600, i.e. it buys stability at a larger effective step. Warmup 1000 → 50 moved the unclipped drop from ~2,130 to ~1,490. Seed-to-seed spread of the drop step is about ±12%.
- **Plateau.** The notes mention that gradient descent can be "super slow in plateau areas … where the gradient is small in all directions". Our loss curves show exactly this: a long plateau at a validation loss ≈ 3.5, then an abrupt fall to ≈ 0.4 (drop at step ~600–900 in the short runs, 3 seeds per setting, see `results/seeds/seeds_drop.png`).
- **What the model computes on the plateau (ours: "audio gap").** The loss with another utterance's input u is equal to the loss with the right input u (gap 0): the model is a (near) **constant function of u**, so on the plateau it only learned the **marginal** distribution of the targets (a language-model-like solution, loss 3.5 against 4.99 without any prefix at all). After the drop it uses u and models the **conditional** distribution P(y | u) (Appendix B). The gap starts to rise about 100 steps before the drop (drop ≈ 1.15 ± 0.04 × the step where the gap reaches 0.3). Since the gap is itself a loss-based quantity this is a warning signal and not an independent predictor.

## 5. Dynamical-systems reading of the drop (Ch. 3, esp. 3.3.4–3.3.5) — carefully hedged
- The learning rule θ(n+1) = θ(n) − μ∇R^emp(θ(n)) is itself a **dynamical system on parameter space**; the plateau is a slow stretch of its trajectory and the drop a sudden qualitative change. The notes describe **bifurcations** as abrupt qualitative changes of a system's behaviour when a **control parameter** passes a critical value, and (3.3.5) note that during training the weights are the control parameters of the network and that the loss curve can show sudden jumps.
- What we can claim: the **timing** of the drop moves with the training settings (learning rate, clipping, batch size, initial scale, penalty strength; no clean law), with seed spread about ±12%.
- What we cannot claim: that this is a bifurcation in the strict sense. We have not identified a fixed point/saddle of the learning dynamics, a critical value of a control parameter, or a change in attractor structure; the notes' own bifurcation examples are about the forward dynamics of recurrent networks, while our projector is feedforward. "Saddle escape" and "phase transition in learning" are our hypotheses, not shown.
- Weight-space measurements (devlog 8–9): the weight change across the drop is carried by few directions in the output layer (low rank), and both layers have to change.

## 6. Representation: text-like or a separate region? (our research question)
- The notes do not treat frozen pre-trained encoders or soft prompts, so this part uses ordinary quantities: vector norms and nearest-neighbour retrieval against chance.
- **Plain projectors (α² = 0).** Projected frames have norm 39–46 against ≈ 0.93 for the LM's word embeddings; word recall (nearest vocabulary token) is ≈ 0 for MLP seeds 1–3 (0.004 / 0.000 / 0.001) and linear seeds 1–3 (0.002 / 0.006 / 0.021). The outputs occupy a **separate region** of the embedding space that the LM learns to read.
- **With the norm penalty.** α² = 0.1: frame norm ≈ 2.3 and word recall 0.329 / 0.336 / 0.318 (three seeds) against a control of ≈ 0.11 (other utterance's transcript words). So penalising the norm makes the representation partly text-like, but at a price in risk (Sec. 3). α² = 0.3 and 1 (just after their drop): recall 0.19 and 0.23.
- **Forcing text-likeness by construction.** Restricting H to a smaller class, H_vocab = convex hull of the word embeddings (the output is a **softmax**-weighted mixture of them, Appendix D), the model **underfits**: validation loss stays at 4.3–4.8 against 0.37 (the "k = 1 polynomial" case of Fig. 2). With logit scale 10 the softmax saturates (entropy 0.002 nats) and learning stalls; this is consistent with vanishing gradients through a saturated softmax, a *hypothesis*, not tested. Neither setting was tuned.
- Conclusion in the notes' words: text-likeness is not necessary to reach low risk (the plain projectors reach 12–19% WER), and imposing it by shrinking the hypothesis space costs the fit; imposing it softly through a penalty trades a worse risk for a more word-like representation.

## 7. Evaluation protocol and its limits (Sec. 1.5–1.7)
- **Splits.** Official LibriSpeech splits are used as S_train / V / S_test. The test set was used only to report, not to choose anything, but **15 models were run on it** whereas the notes ask that S_test be visited once for the final model; this should be stated.
- **No k-fold cross-validation.** Hyperparameters (learning rate, warmup, clipping, α², init scale, batch size) were explored one factor at a time on V, and the best-validation checkpoint was restored (**early stopping** in the sense of Sec. 1.6 / 2.2.3 for expensive training; we did not stop early, we kept the best step). The notes allow this when a training run is expensive. We also did not retrain a final model on the whole training split.
- **Error bars.** Random initialization (and minibatch order) make the trained model a random variable; the plots show mean ± sample standard deviation over **3 seeds** (crude for n = 3) for the baseline, MLP and penalty 0.1 groups. WER intervals are 95% **bootstrap** intervals over test utterances (sampling variability of the test risk estimate). Everything else (lr tests, init scale, penalties 0.3 / 1 / 10, the 12,000-step run, vocab runs) is 1–2 seeds and should be called preliminary.
- **Test WER, summary** (500 utterances): Whisper-small 4.5%; linear 20.3% ± 0.8; MLP 18.8% ± 0.4; MLP + α² = 0.1 29.6% ± 7.9; MLP 12,000 steps 12.3% (single seed); untrained projector 117–169%.
- **Error analysis** (12,000-step model): substitutions 9.2%, insertions 1.8%, deletions 1.3% of reference words; 26% of utterances without error; errors are mostly acoustically plausible confusions (AND↔IN, THE↔A, names and spelling variants). The 1,500-step MLP also shows a few runaway repetitions (0.4% of utterances) that inflate the corpus WER.

## 8. Where the notes' framework stops (say so in the report)
- Frozen pre-trained components, soft prompts and the "separate region" question are outside the notes.
- The drop is described with notes-style vocabulary (plateau, performance surface, bifurcation) but we did not establish a bifurcation.
- The generalisation story rests on a small test set (500 utterances), one data subset, and few seeds.
